01 · Google Maps

Road Safety & Awareness

Exploring how navigation could move beyond directions to help people understand changing road conditions, hazards and comfort before they reach them.

Visual report · Full thinking→
Open visual report
The opportunity

Knowing the route isn't the same as understanding the road.

Maps are excellent at telling us where to go. The missing layer is what the road itself may demand: a sudden hazard, rough surface, uncomfortable route or local safety concern.

Safer. Smoother. More aware.Product direction
01 · Problem

Drivers can be surprised by unmarked speed breakers, potholes, rough surfaces, diversions and unfamiliar conditions.

02 · Users
  • Everyday drivers
  • Unfamiliar-area travellers
  • Comfort-sensitive passengers
  • Drivers seeking less screen attention
03 · Insight

Traffic-aware routing solves movement. A safety layer should solve anticipation.

04 · Opportunity

Turn community, sensor and public-data signals into simple, confidence-building warnings and route choices.

05 · Proposed product
SafePathHazard intelligence
Comfort RouteSmoother routing
VisionDriveVisual navigation
06 · MVP
  • Verified hazard alerts
  • Audio + visual warnings
  • Fast reporting
  • Optional comfort route
07 · Success
  • Alert usefulness
  • Route adoption
  • Fewer surprise events
  • Repeat use
08 · Risks

False alarms, stale reports, privacy, distraction and uncertain local-safety information presented as fact. Use confidence labels and low-distraction delivery.

Problem → insight → opportunity → solution → MVP → measurement
Deeper thinking

Anticipation, not alarm.

The key product tension is information versus attention. More warnings are not automatically safer. Surface a warning when it can change the next decision and stay quiet when the signal is weak.

UsefulWarn when information changes a decision.
QuietStay backgrounded when the signal is weak.

Next experiment

Prototype realistic driving scenarios and test whether users can recognise, trust and act on the warning without increasing distraction.

02 · Alexa+

Making Voice Interaction Visual

Exploring how an expressive visual layer could make an ambient home assistant easier to understand, more engaging and more useful without taking away the simplicity of voice.

Visual report · Full thinking→
Open visual report
The opportunity

Voice works. Some moments need more than voice.

Voice is fast, natural and hands-free. Complex information, learning, context and shared household interactions can benefit from a visual layer.

Voice + visual contextProduct direction
01 · Problem

Audio-only responses can be hard to scan, remember or understand when information becomes complex.

02 · Users
  • Voice-assistant households
  • Families and children
  • Complex-question users
  • People needing visual context
03 · Insight

The opportunity is not “make Alexa a hologram.” It is make the right responses visible when visibility adds value.

04 · Opportunity

Create an ambient visual companion that appears contextually: expressive when useful, quiet when not.

05 · Proposed product
Visual responsesCards + context
PresenceSimple expression
Household contextLearning + routines
06 · MVP
  • Visual response layer
  • Contextual cards
  • Simple companion state
  • Core household tasks
07 · Success
  • Comprehension
  • Task completion
  • Engagement
  • Repeat use
08 · Risks

Novelty without utility, visual distraction, privacy, hardware cost and an avatar becoming more important than the task.

Observe → question → investigate → define → explore → prioritize → measure
Deeper thinking

The avatar is not the product.

The product outcome is better understanding. Voice should remain the default for quick commands; visuals should appear when they improve comprehension, comparison, demonstration or shared attention.

Voice-firstKeep simple commands simple.
Visual when valuableUse context only when it improves the task.

Next experiment

Compare the same task in three modes: voice only, voice + static visual and voice + expressive companion.

03 · SkyServe

Rethinking Airport Delivery

Bringing airport food, retail and essentials to the passenger instead of asking the passenger to navigate the terminal for every need.

Visual report · Full thinking→
Open visual report
The opportunity

Don't make the passenger walk for every need.

Airports are time-sensitive environments. The product can bring the terminal's services to the passenger instead.

Discover · order · deliverService loop
01 · Problem

Long walks, queues, unfamiliar layouts, limited time and fragmented retail discovery create friction.

02 · Users
  • Passengers at gates
  • Short-connection travellers
  • Families / accessibility-sensitive users
  • Frequent flyers
03 · Insight

The airport is already a marketplace. The missing layer connects where the passenger is with what the terminal can provide.

04 · Opportunity

Combine geo-fenced discovery, ordering and gate-aware delivery into one controlled service.

05 · Service model
DiscoverNearby vendors
OrderDelivery / pickup
DeliverGate-aware handoff
06 · MVP
  • One terminal pilot
  • Limited vendors
  • Zone detection
  • Order tracking
07 · Business model
  • Vendor commission
  • Delivery fee
  • Sponsored discovery
  • Subscription later
08 · Risks

Delivery reliability, restricted zones, security rules, vendor integration, late orders and unclear handoffs.

Passenger pain → service loop → MVP → operations → economics
Deeper thinking

The hardest problem isn't the app.

Airport operations are the constraint. Security zones, vendor readiness, runner access and boarding-time uncertainty can break a beautiful consumer flow.

Next experiment

Run a manual concierge pilot and measure order willingness, delivery accuracy and failed handoffs before building the full marketplace.

04 · Kitchen-Genie

An Intelligent Kitchen Companion

Reducing meal-planning friction, forgotten ingredients and everyday kitchen decision fatigue through a connected AI companion.

Visual report · Full thinking→
Open visual report
The opportunity

“What should I cook?” is often an information problem.

People already have ingredients, preferences, time constraints and routines. The friction comes from connecting those pieces into a useful decision.

Plan less. Use more. Waste less.Product promise
01 · Problem

Meal decisions create cognitive load, ingredients get forgotten and shopping can duplicate what is already at home.

02 · Users
  • Busy home cooks
  • Shared households
  • Waste-conscious users
  • Time / diet constrained users
03 · Insight

The useful unit is not a recipe. It is the next kitchen decision.

04 · Opportunity

Connect inventory, preferences, time and recipes into one planning loop.

05 · Product loop
KnowWhat is available
DecideWhat fits today
ActCook · shop · update
06 · MVP
  • Inventory capture
  • Expiry awareness
  • Meal suggestions
  • Shopping list
07 · Business model
  • Useful free core
  • Premium household tier
  • Partner commerce later
08 · Risks

Inventory accuracy, notification fatigue, privacy, recipe quality and building too much automation before trust exists.

Decision fatigue → connected context → useful next action
Deeper thinking

The product should answer the next decision.

Instead of asking users to maintain a perfect digital pantry, the assistant should earn trust by being useful even with incomplete information.

What can I make?Use available ingredients and time.
What should I use first?Surface ingredients likely to expire.

Next experiment

Test the “What can I make tonight?” flow with real household inventories for a week and measure planning time and actual cooking decisions.

Beyond technology

As a national-level netball player and school sports captain, I learned that leadership is often less about having the strongest individual player and more about understanding the team you actually have.

When several of our strongest players moved into a higher age group, I had to give younger players unfamiliar responsibilities. Rather than reproducing the old strategy, I redistributed roles around their strengths and focused on confidence as much as execution.

That lesson still shapes how I approach product problems: understand the constraints, work with the people and resources that actually exist, and adapt the strategy without losing sight of the outcome.
